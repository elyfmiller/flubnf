Methods

Purpose

FluBNF generates real-time probabilistic forecasts of weekly influenza hospital admissions for all 52 US jurisdictions, in the quantile format required by the CDC FluSight forecast hub. Two models run each week and each is submitted on its own. The Oracle SIHRS is a mechanistic forecast: the SIHRS compartment model, fitted each week by a sequential particle filter, whose forecast growth is then blended with donor growth from past seasons at the same calendar week. The Groundhog is a calendar analogue built from historical seasons with an auxiliary donor bank. Both draw on past seasons at the same point in the calendar, in different ways; the two are submitted separately and nothing is blended between them.

The SIHRS compartment model

The SIHRS compartment model is the model of influenza transmission the Oracle SIHRS is built on. Individuals move from Susceptible to Infected; most infections recover directly, while a small severe fraction is admitted to Hospitalized before reaching Recovered. Immunity wanes, so recovered individuals eventually return to the susceptible pool. The model is written in BNGL, the BioNetGen model-specification language.

{{ dg.sihrs(uid='mm') }}

Rates. Transmission β(t) varies over the season as a smooth annual harmonic, so the model can produce the winter wave without hand-set breakpoints. Infections resolve at rate γ (fixed from the measured influenza generation time); a fixed severe fraction ρ (the infection-hospitalization ratio) is admitted, and the remaining (1-ρ) recover directly. Hospitalized individuals discharge at γₕ, and immunity wanes at ω, returning people to the susceptible pool.

{{ dg.eq_pf() }}

Why this structure. Three reasons, each tied to the forecasting target. First, hospital admissions are the FluSight target, so H is an explicit compartment: the model fits the actual I to H admission flux, scaled by a fitted reporting ascertainment, rather than a scaled proxy of infections. Second, waning immunity (R back to S) permits repeat seasonal waves, which single-pass SIR-type structures cannot produce. Third, seasonal transmission lets the data determine when transmissibility peaks in each state. Five parameters are fitted per state. Of the fixed values, the recovery rate traces to a cited generation-time study, while ρ, γH and ω are working assumptions with no source behind them; the provenance module (flubnf/sihrs_priors.py) records each value's status, including which ones are assumptions.

Fitting: a sequential particle filter

FluBNF fits the SIHRS compartment model with a particle filter, implemented as fit_type=pf in a fork of PyBNF, Los Alamos National Laboratory's parameter-fitting suite for BioNetGen models. The filter maintains 10,000 candidate epidemics per state, each a complete trajectory of the compartment model with its own parameters. Every week the newest admissions reweight the candidates by how well they explain the data; a Liu-West kernel then jitters the surviving parameters at scale 0.15 (chosen by a pre-registered sweep), the log-uniform ascertainment and dispersion on the log scale and the transmission parameters on their own scale, and systematic resampling keeps the population diverse. The spread of surviving trajectories is the forecast uncertainty. Each week's forecast is a fresh filter run from the season's August 1 anchor through that week's data vintage, never a cloud carried over from the week before: a refit sees every revision to earlier weeks, and the carried-cloud alternative was measured and declined. The filter's forecast is not submitted as it stands: the Oracle step below blends its growth with past seasons' before anything is submitted.

BioNetGen's BNG2.pl is invoked once per state cell when a run is prepared, expanding the BNGL model into its reaction network. During fitting, the particle filter integrates that network's differential equations with bngsim, an in-process BioNetGen simulation engine: no per-simulation BNG2.pl subprocess is launched, so a full state-season fit completes in seconds.

The Oracle step: past seasons' growth, blended in

{{ dg.oracle_pipeline(uid='mp') }}

What it does. After the weekly fit, the filter's forecast is a set of sample paths for the next four weeks (30,000 per state at the default three replicates), with one growth rate for the filter at the forecast origin. The Oracle step gives every sample path one donor: a growth path drawn at random from an earlier season at the same calendar week (within two MMWR epiweeks of the forecast week, from any jurisdiction), the same calendar-donor principle the Groundhog uses for its donor ratios. The path's growth over each of the four forecast weeks becomes the geometric mean, half and half, of the filter's own growth and its donor's. The blended growth is propagated in closed form from the filter's own current state, so each sample is rescaled rather than refitted: the filter's uncertainty rides on the new path and the donors add the spread of how past seasons moved. One donor per sample, not one donor per forecast, is what keeps both.

Donors. {{ oracle_text.BANK_TEXT.pool }}

Why. A particle filter tracks the season it has seen so far, and its forecast carries the growth it sees today forward. That is right while a season keeps its course and wrong at the turns, which the season so far cannot show. Past seasons at the same calendar week show how seasons have moved on from this point of the year. The blend keeps the filter's reading of the current state and borrows the calendar's knowledge of what usually comes next.

How it relates to the Groundhog. Both models read past seasons at the same calendar week. The Groundhog applies donor growth ratios to the last observed count and has no mechanism at all; the Oracle SIHRS applies donor growth to the mechanistic model's own fitted state, at half weight beside the filter's own. The two are submitted as separate models, each under its own hub identity, and nothing is blended between them.

The record. Every rule of the step (the weight, one donor per sample path, the pool rule, five seeds logged with the first submitted) was frozen by a pre-registration before the step itself was ever scored (sha256 {{ oracle_text.PREREG_SHA256[:16] }}), and the shipped donor bank (its two streams, the half-and-half draw, the shrink) by a second one before that bank was scored (sha256 {{ oracle_text.B2_SHA256[:16] }}; docs/ORACLE-SIHRS.md). Research: the two-strain variant not a shipped model

What it does. The two-strain SIHRS variant models influenza A and influenza B as independent transmission circuits, each with its own seasonally varying transmission rate and its own peak timing. Each circuit carries its own susceptible, hospitalized, and recovered pools spanning the whole population, so infection with one strain confers no protection against the other. Observed admissions are the ascertained sum of the two circuits' admission fluxes, which lets the model represent seasons in which an early A wave is followed by a late B wave.

{{ dg.sihrs2(uid='m2') }}

A second observation channel. Fitting adds a second likelihood channel to the particle filter. In addition to weekly NHSN admissions, each candidate epidemic is weighed by weekly typed positives from NREVSS clinical laboratories, treated as binomial counts of influenza A among typed specimens, so the filter learns the A/B mix as well as the admissions level. The initial A/B mix at the season start is also set from this typed surveillance series rather than fitted. Both channels use vintage data; typed counts enter only through the week preceding the forecast date, matching the NREVSS release cadence.

{{ dg.eq_pf2s() }}

Why it does not ship. The two-strain member is stronger than the single-strain filter at turning points and weaker on the full grid: when it was evaluated (August 2026, three replayed seasons) it scored worse than the single-strain model overall, and the turning-point gain did not pay for that. It remains a research run.

The engine remains available for research runs. The two-strain model page stays reachable by direct URL, and the run endpoint still accepts a three-member request, so the variant can be scored and developed further without appearing in the operational path.

The Groundhog

{{ dg.analogue_ratios(uid='ma') }}

The Groundhog is a purely empirical model with no epidemiological mechanism, submitted on its own. For a forecast at horizon h it collects donor weeks: every week from strictly earlier seasons, in any state, whose MMWR epiweek falls within two weeks of the forecast date's. For each donor it records the growth ratio between admissions h weeks after the donor week and admissions in the donor week itself. The forecast distribution is the last observed value scaled by the empirical quantiles of those growth ratios; a cell with fewer than 30 usable donors produces no forecast. Donors are pooled across states because a single state supplies only about five donors per prior season inside the calendar window, far short of the 30-donor minimum, and they never come from the season being forecast. The pool also holds the US national row, about 2 percent of donors, measured 2026-09-08 and kept; the release record has the numbers. Epiweek 53, which MMWR years such as 2014, 2020 and 2025 carry, is seated between weeks 52 and 1 for the calendar match, and a donor's future value is always read by date arithmetic, never by week label.

The auxiliary donor bank. A second pool is built by the same construction on FluSurv-NET weekly hospitalization rates, banked in the repository as a content-addressed file whose digest every run records (the stream has no revision history, so a snapshot is the only thing there is). Its growth ratios are shrunk toward the admissions scale by a factor fitted only on strictly earlier overlap seasons, and the two ratio quantile functions are averaged at a fixed equal weight that is not fitted. This is what separates the Groundhog from the bare calendar analogue: the same engine, the same admissions pool, plus these donors. The arm was pre-registered and selected against an ILI+ bank on the same cells; both banks are committed and either can be run.

Coverage. The construction is split-conformal in form only. The calendar windows overlap and the donors are neither exchangeable with the target nor independent of one another (weekly values are serially dependent, one donor week serves five windows, jurisdictions move together within a season), so no finite-sample coverage guarantee is claimed. Band-conditional empirical coverage is measured from the replays instead: over three seasons the central 50, 80 and 90 percent bands covered 0.51, 0.80 and 0.95, with the worst band deviation 0.012.

One prior season is excluded from the donor pool: 2021-22. It peaked at epiweek 16, in April 2022, while every other donor season peaked between epiweek 48 and epiweek 6, and the archived surveillance series begins 2022-02-05, so that season survives only as its growth phase. A calendar-matched pool asks what happened in March and 2021-22 answers that the epidemic was still growing: it is the only donor season whose March ratios have a median above one, with a far heavier upper tail than any other season's. Excluding it was pre-registered and measured before it shipped (August 2026): the improvement held in all three replayed seasons and in 50 of 52 jurisdictions.

Measured performance

Three seasons replayed in real-time conditions: each week the models saw only that week's archived data vintage, submitted, and moved on. Scores are relative weighted interval scores (relWIS) against the official FluSight baseline, ratio of sums; values below 1 beat the baseline. The convention is stated in full under Data and verification policies below.

Data and verification policies

Every run uses vintage data: the data exactly as archived on its forecast date, never revised history. Every run is seeded and reproducible from its ledger entry. Missing weeks are never imputed; they are dropped as rows with calendar-true offsets. All scoring uses the weighted interval score against the official FluSight baseline, the competition's own yardstick, and evaluation touches only data that was available at forecast time.

Two measured qualifications, itemized in the release record's seal caveats (docs/archive/RELEASE-1.0.md): for seven of the 76 sealed as-of weeks the archived vintage differs from what was public by that week's submission deadline, and the sealed scores are computed on the members' internal forecasts at float precision rather than on the rounded submission files, a measured difference at the third decimal.

{# the scored-cell rule lives here: this card survives the site harvest #}

A scored cell is one location, one forecast week, one horizon. A cell counts only where settled truth exists and is positive, the forecast median is positive, and the FluSight baseline also covers that cell. Official FluSight scoring keeps cells this rule drops, so a count here can differ from a count published there.

{# here, not in Measured performance: the site harvest drops that card, and a withdrawn claim must stay withdrawn in both places #}

Placement against the full FluSight field, meaning a rank among all submitting teams, was withdrawn on 2026-08-24 and is not restated: a rank would assert that every submitting team was scored end to end on one stated convention, and the field's scores come from a builder that lives outside this repository and cannot be rerun from it. Until the field and this project's entry are scored that way, no placement is claimed anywhere in this application; the full account is in the release record (docs/archive/RELEASE-1.0.md).
